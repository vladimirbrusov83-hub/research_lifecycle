# Product

## Register

brand

## Users
Researchers and academics — grad students, faculty, and early-career researchers navigating the research pipeline, plus librarians who support them. They're reading this reflectively (not mid-task), often via a shared link, to understand how their own workflow splits between human judgment, AI assistance, and library infrastructure.

## Product Purpose
An interactive visual essay mapping the ten phases of the research lifecycle (problem framing through post-publication impact), showing who carries the load at each phase: the researcher (judgment), agentic AI (velocity/automation), and the academic library (trust, access, permanence). Success is a reader coming away with a clear, honest mental model of where AI genuinely helps, where it doesn't, and where the library's role is non-negotiable — not a hype piece for AI tools.

## Brand Personality
Field guide, not dashboard. Printed, considered, quietly authoritative — like a well-made reference book, not a SaaS product page. Warm paper aesthetic, serif display type, editorial restraint over flashy chrome.

## Anti-references
Not a SaaS landing page. Not a slide-deck-to-web conversion. Avoid dashboard/app-shell visual language (card grids, metric tiles, sidebar nav) — this is long-form reading content, not a tool. Avoid overselling AI; the honest tension (AI output as "suggestion" vs. "record that must stand up in review") is the point, not a caveat to soften.

## Design Principles
- Read like a printed field guide, not a web app — editorial pacing over dashboard density.
- Show the honest tension at each phase (where lanes overlap, where AI is unreliable) rather than smoothing it into marketing copy.
- Motion and interactivity (load-shift curve, Library Lens, reveal-on-scroll) should clarify structure, not decorate.
- Respect the reader's attention: this is meant to be read end to end, not skimmed for a CTA.

## Accessibility & Inclusion
Respects `prefers-reduced-motion`. No stated WCAG target beyond that; treat body-text contrast and focus states as baseline requirements given the reading-heavy nature of the content.
